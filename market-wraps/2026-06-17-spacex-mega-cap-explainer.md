# How did a rocket company briefly become more valuable than Microsoft?

---

## 1. Hook: Why SpaceX is on everyone's feed today

If your social feed looks anything like Wall Street's trading screens right now, one name is impossible to ignore: **SpaceX**.

In the span of roughly one week, Elon Musk's company went from the largest initial public offering in history to something even more surreal — a stock that **briefly surpassed Microsoft** in market value on Tuesday, June 17, 2026, before settling as one of the five most valuable companies on Earth. At its intraday peak, SpaceX's valuation touched roughly **$2.94 trillion**, according to CNBC, edging past Microsoft's roughly **$2.93 trillion** before pulling back.

That is not a typo, and it is not a slow climb over years. The shares have surged about **62%** from the IPO price of **$135**, CNBC reported — a gain larger than Amazon's roughly **45%** rise over the past five years. The rally made Musk the world's **first trillionaire**, minted a wave of new millionaires inside the company, and turned a space-and-satellite business into the hottest mega-cap story on the planet.

And then, as if the IPO were not enough drama, SpaceX announced on the same day that it would acquire **Cursor**, a popular AI coding startup, in an all-stock deal valued at **$60 billion** — one of the largest software acquisitions ever, signed just days after going public.

This is the story investors, skeptics, and scrollers are all trying to decode at once.

---

## 2. What happened (facts + numbers)

**The IPO that rewrote the record books.** SpaceX priced its offering at **$135 per share** on **555.6 million** Class A shares, raising **$75 billion** and valuing the company at roughly **$1.77 trillion**, according to its investor relations release and CNBC. That topped Saudi Aramco's 2019 listing as the largest IPO on record. The stock began trading on the Nasdaq on **Friday, June 12, 2026**, opening at **$150** and closing its first session up **19%**. The company later closed the full offering — including the underwriters' overallotment — on **June 15**, bringing total gross proceeds to about **$85.7 billion** on **638.9 million** shares sold.

**A vertical rally in public trading.** After debut day, the stock kept rising. CNBC reported that SpaceX shares jumped **20% on Monday, June 16**, in their first full day of trading after the blockbuster debut. On **Tuesday, June 17**, shares rose about **4%** as the rally extended into a third consecutive session, according to CNBC and Seoul Economic Daily.

**Market cap milestones that stunned the market.** Tuesday's surge pushed SpaceX's valuation to an intraday peak of about **$2.94 trillion**, briefly making it the **fourth-largest U.S. company by market value**, ahead of Microsoft, CNBC reported. By the close, the valuation had retreated to about **$2.65 trillion** — still enough to leapfrog **Amazon**, which sat near **$2.64 trillion**. Apple remained in third place with a market value of nearly **$4.4 trillion**, a much higher bar.

**The Cursor deal lands.** On **June 16**, SpaceX filed with the SEC that it agreed to acquire **Anysphere**, the company behind the AI coding tool Cursor, in an all-stock merger valuing Cursor's equity at **$60 billion**. The deal, expected to close in the **third quarter of 2026**, subject to regulatory approval, follows an unusual April arrangement in which SpaceX had committed to either buy Cursor or pay a **$10 billion** breakup fee, according to TechCrunch and The Verge.

**Musk raises the stakes on growth.** On Sunday, Musk posted on X that SpaceX "might be able to reach approximately" **$1 trillion in revenue by 2030**, CNBC noted — a figure that would represent a massive leap from **$18.7 billion** in 2025 revenue disclosed in the company's prospectus.

**The financial reality underneath the hype.** SpaceX reported a **$4.9 billion net loss** in 2025 and lost **$4.28 billion** in the first quarter of 2026, according to CNBC. Research firm CFRA initiated coverage with a **sell** rating and a **12-month price target of $115**, calling the valuation extreme relative to the company's capital intensity and growth assumptions.

## 3. The business, explained like you're at a coffee shop

Imagine a company that runs **three very different businesses under one roof**, and only one of them reliably makes money today.

**The rocket side** is what most people picture first. SpaceX launches satellites and cargo into orbit, reuses its rockets to cut costs, and holds contracts with NASA and the U.S. Department of Defense. Think of it as owning the **trucks and highways** of space — essential infrastructure, but expensive to build and operate. This segment generated about **$4.1 billion** in revenue in 2025 and posted an operating loss of **$657 million**, according to CNBC's review of IPO filings.

**Starlink is the subscription business.** SpaceX beams internet from a constellation of thousands of satellites to homes, ships, airlines, and remote regions. It is the company's **profit engine**: Starlink brought in **$11.39 billion** in revenue in 2025 — about **61%** of total sales — and generated **$4.42 billion** in operating income, CNBC reported. Subscriber count reached **10.3 million** in the first quarter of 2026. Picture a global Wi-Fi company that builds its own delivery trucks (rockets) — that vertical integration is Starlink's edge, but also why average revenue per user has been drifting lower as the company pushes into more price-sensitive markets.

**The AI layer is the new, expensive bet.** In **February 2026**, SpaceX merged with Musk's artificial intelligence startup **xAI**, folding in the Grok chatbot and related infrastructure. The AI division lost **$6.35 billion** in 2025, CNBC reported. The company now describes itself as a **"space AI compute infrastructure"** business — rockets plus satellites plus AI data centers — with a total addressable market it estimates in the tens of trillions of dollars. The **Cursor acquisition** fits here: SpaceX is buying a tool that helps software developers write code with AI, aiming to compete with OpenAI and Anthropic in enterprise software.

So when you buy SpaceX stock at nearly **$3 trillion**, you are not buying a simple rocket company. You are buying a bundle: a profitable satellite internet service, a capital-hungry launch business, and a loss-making AI platform that management believes will define the next decade.

## 4. Why the market reacted this way

**Scarcity plus momentum.** SpaceX was the most anticipated IPO in years, with demand reportedly exceeding **$250 billion** against a **$75 billion** offer — roughly **four times oversubscribed**, according to IPO filings cited by financial media. When a company that large, that famous, and that hard to access finally opens to public buyers, the first days of trading become less about spreadsheets and more about **who gets in**. That creates a feedback loop: rising prices attract headlines, which attract more buyers.

**The "everything company" premium.** Investors are not just pricing rockets. They are pricing a vision that combines **Starlink's recurring revenue**, **Starship's future launch economics**, and **AI infrastructure** into one conglomerate led by the world's most closely watched CEO. Dan Ives of Wedbush told CNBC that investors are focused on a "fourth industrial revolution" spanning space, infrastructure, and AI — a narrative that traditional price-to-sales math struggles to contain.

**Post-IPO currency makes big deals possible.** The Cursor acquisition is partly a story about **using a soaring stock as currency**. TechCrunch noted that since the IPO, SpaceX's rising valuation added nearly **$1 trillion** in market cap in just a few days — equivalent to many times the Cursor purchase price. Buying a **$60 billion** AI coding company with stock looks very different when your shares are up **62%** in a week.

**A market rotating away from crowded chip trades.** On the same day SpaceX rallied, semiconductor stocks sold off sharply and the Nasdaq fell **1.15%**, while the Dow hit a record. Some fund managers had already flagged chips as the most crowded trade in history, according to Bank of America's June survey. SpaceX offered a fresh, narrative-driven mega-cap story at the exact moment investors were looking to redeploy attention — and cash — elsewhere.

**Skeptics exist, but they are not driving the first week.** CFRA's **sell** rating and **$115** target reflect concern that the stock prices perfection. Steve Westly, a former Tesla board member, told CNBC that investors may turn "grumpy" if growth projections in the prospectus are not met within a few quarters. But in the opening sessions, bullish momentum and limited float overwhelmed those voices.

## 5. Risks / what could change the story

**Valuation versus reality.** At its peak, SpaceX traded at a market value many times its **$18.7 billion** in trailing revenue — a multiple far above even the richest large-cap technology names. If growth slows or losses widen, the stock has a long way to fall. Morningstar has pegged fair value far below the trading price, though that figure should be treated as one analyst view among many.

**Starlink's growth is getting harder.** CNBC reported that average revenue per user has declined as Starlink expands into more competitive, urban markets. Operating income barely moved in the first quarter even as subscribers doubled — a sign that growth is becoming more expensive to acquire.

**The AI bet burns cash fast.** xAI's losses and heavy capital spending are a drag on consolidated results. The Cursor deal adds integration risk and regulatory scrutiny. If enterprise customers stick with established AI labs, SpaceX's software pivot may not deliver the revenue Musk is implying.

**Lock-up and insider selling.** Standard IPO lock-up periods eventually expire, potentially adding supply to the market. Musk's enormous personal stake also raises governance and concentration questions that matter at this scale.

**Geopolitical and operational risk.** Starlink's global footprint, defense contracts, and space launch operations all carry regulatory and mission-failure risk. A major launch setback or policy shift could hit sentiment quickly — even for a stock that, as one CNBC commentator put it, sometimes trades as if it lives on "its own planet."

**The Fed and rates.** High-growth, high-multiple names are sensitive to interest-rate expectations. With the Federal Reserve meeting on **June 18**, any hawkish surprise could pressure the kind of long-duration growth story SpaceX represents.

## 6. One open question for viewers

**Can a company that lost nearly $5 billion last year justify a valuation that briefly topped Microsoft — or was Tuesday's peak the market's excitement talking, not its math?**

Watch the next few earnings reports, Starlink subscriber economics, and whether the Cursor deal closes smoothly. The answer will tell you if SpaceX is building a new category of mega-cap company — or living through one of the wildest first weeks in IPO history.
