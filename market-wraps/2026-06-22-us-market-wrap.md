# Why Did the Nasdaq Fall Over 1% While the Dow Rose on Monday?

Wall Street returned from a three-day weekend on Monday, June 22, 2026, and the closing bell told a split story. The Dow Jones Industrial Average managed a modest gain as oil prices eased and industrial and healthcare stocks held up. The Nasdaq Composite, meanwhile, dropped more than 1% as a wave of selling swept through the market's largest technology companies. The S&P 500 sat in the middle — down slightly — pulled lower by the sheer weight of a few megacap declines even as most of the market's sectors actually finished higher.

The clearest narrative of the day is not geopolitics alone and not inflation alone. It is **investors questioning who really wins the artificial-intelligence spending boom**. Alphabet plunged after another high-profile AI researcher announced he was leaving for a rival. Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft fell alongside it as worries about massive infrastructure budgets collided with doubts about near-term returns. At the same time, chip makers, banks, and industrial names advanced — a rotation that left major indexes pointing in different directions on the same afternoon.

## What happened (bullet facts with numbers)

- The **S&P 500** fell **27.79 points**, or **0.4%**, to close at **7,472.79**, according to the Associated Press. The benchmark now sits about **1.8%** below its all-time high reached earlier in June.
- The **Dow Jones Industrial Average** rose **148.01 points**, or **0.3%**, to **51,712.71**.
- The **Nasdaq Composite** dropped **351.33 points**, or **1.3%**, to **26,166.60** — the day's weakest major index.
- The **Russell 2000** index of smaller companies gained **24.64 points**, or **0.8%**, to **3,004.40**, showing that weakness was concentrated in large-cap tech rather than across the full market.
- **Eight of the 11 major S&P 500 sectors closed higher**, per session reporting from multiple outlets. **Financials** and **industrials** each rose about **0.7%**, while **healthcare** added roughly **1%**.
- **Communication services** was the weakest sector, falling about **4%**, dragged down by sharp losses in Alphabet and other large platform companies, according to Reuters session notes.
- **Alphabet** shares fell roughly **5% to 6%** — the stock's largest intraday decline since February, per Bloomberg — after Google DeepMind vice president John Jumper said he was leaving for Anthropic. It followed the recent departure of prominent researcher Noam Shazeer to OpenAI.
- **Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft** each declined between roughly **2% and 4%**, extending pressure across the megacap technology group.
- **SpaceX** fell about **14% to 16%**, marking its third consecutive down session, after disclosing plans for its first-ever public bond offering. The company reported holding about **$100.8 billion** in cash and cash equivalents as of June 19.
- **Semiconductor stocks diverged from software.** The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index rose about **1%** to a record high, with Micron gaining roughly **4% to 5%** and Sandisk up about **5.4%** ahead of Micron's earnings later in the week.
- **AbbVie** agreed to acquire **Apogee Therapeutics** for **$10.9 billion** in cash. Apogee shares surged nearly **47%**, and AbbVie rose about **6.7%**.
- **Oil prices fell roughly 3%** as U.S.–Iran negotiations in Switzerland showed progress toward a roadmap for a final deal within **60 days**, according to the Associated Press and Reuters. Brent crude settled near **$77.61 to $77.90** a barrel, down about **3%**.
- **Treasury yields climbed**, with the **10-year yield** moving to about **4.51%**, per Reuters — pressuring long-duration growth stocks whose valuations depend heavily on distant future earnings.
- Markets were closed Friday, **June 19**, for the **Juneteenth** federal holiday, so Monday was the first session to absorb weekend news on diplomacy, corporate filings, and positioning ahead of key inflation data later in the week.
- **Volatility** remained in a moderate range; specific closing VIX data for June 22 was not yet uniformly reported across primary sources at the time of publication — **data pending verification** for the official session close.

## Why it moved (3 short points, everyday analogies OK)

**1. Alphabet's AI talent exits reopened the "spending vs. payoff" debate.**

When two of your most visible AI researchers leave for direct competitors within days, investors do not just notice the headlines — they rethink the timeline for winning the technology race. John Jumper, a Nobel laureate who helped lead work at Google DeepMind, announced he was joining Anthropic. Noam Shazeer had already said he was moving to OpenAI. Analysts cited concerns that Google may be losing ground in the competition for frontier AI talent.

That matters because Alphabet and its peers are committing enormous sums to data centers, chips, and compute capacity — capital spending guidance for 2026 has been cited in the range of **$180 billion to $190 billion** for Alphabet alone. The market's reaction on Monday was less about one departure and more about a growing question: if the people building the models are walking out the door, will all that spending convert into revenue fast enough? Think of a restaurant chain pouring money into new kitchens while its head chefs take jobs at the competition next door. Investors started repricing that risk immediately.

**2. The market rotated from "AI spenders" toward "AI suppliers" and old-economy leaders.**

Monday's trading looked like a textbook sector rotation, not a broad panic. Banks and industrial companies — names like JPMorgan Chase and Caterpillar — helped lift the Dow. Healthcare deal-making added fuel, with AbbVie's acquisition of Apogee reinforcing that merger activity remains alive in biotech.

Meanwhile, chip and memory companies rallied even as the companies funding the AI buildout sold off. Bloomberg noted that investors were effectively selling the hyperscalers spending on compute while buying the suppliers receiving those dollars — Micron climbed as much as **6.2%** intraday. The Nasdaq fell not because technology broadly collapsed, but because a handful of enormous index weights dropped hard enough to outweigh gains elsewhere. With eight sectors in the green, the equal-weight version of the market told a calmer story than the cap-weighted headline indexes.

**3. Falling oil helped the Dow, but rising yields kept pressure on growth stocks.**

Progress in U.S.–Iran talks acted like a partial refund on the energy shock that has fed inflation worries since the conflict escalated. Mediators described encouraging progress in Switzerland, and oil slid about **3%** — easing one source of cost pressure for consumers and manufacturers. That supported cyclical Dow components tied to travel, industry, and the broader economy.

But the bond market did not fully join the equity relief rally. Yields rose anyway, reflecting lingering concern that the Federal Reserve may need to keep policy restrictive — or even hike rates later this year — if inflation does not cool quickly enough. Higher yields raise the discount rate on future profits, which hits long-growth stocks hardest. That is a key reason the Nasdaq underperformed even on a day when oil fell and diplomacy improved: macro anxiety about rates did not disappear; it just competed with geopolitical good news.

## What to watch next (2-3 objective bullets, not trade advice)

- **Thursday's release of the May Personal Consumption Expenditures price index** is the week's most important inflation reading. The PCE report is the Federal Reserve's preferred gauge, and economists expect core inflation may have ticked higher from April levels, according to FactSet data cited by multiple outlets. A hotter-than-expected number could reinforce expectations for a rate hike later this year; a cooler print could ease the pressure on growth stocks.
- **Wednesday's Micron earnings report** will serve as a live test of whether AI-driven demand for memory chips still has fundamental momentum. The company enters the week with shares up sharply for the year, and guidance will be watched as a signal for the broader semiconductor complex — the part of the market that rallied while megacap platforms sold off on Monday.
- **The next phase of U.S.–Iran diplomacy** remains a live variable for oil, inflation, and global risk appetite. Negotiators outlined a **60-day roadmap** toward a final deal, but tensions around Lebanon and the Strait of Hormuz have not fully disappeared. Any reversal in talks could quickly lift energy prices again and complicate the inflation picture the Fed is watching.

Monday did not break the market's year-long uptrend, but it sharpened an emerging fault line: investors are still betting on artificial intelligence, yet they are increasingly choosy about where that bet lives. The companies building the infrastructure are being rewarded. The companies funding the buildout at historic scale are being asked to prove the bill will be worth it — and on Monday, the market's answer was skeptical enough to send the Nasdaq down more than 1% while the Dow quietly rose.
