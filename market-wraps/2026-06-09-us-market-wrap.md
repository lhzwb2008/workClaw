# Why Did Wall Street Bounce — but Only in Chips?

Wall Street ended Monday, June 8, 2026, with a split-screen recovery. The headline indexes that investors watch every day moved higher, but the rally was narrow, fast, and heavily concentrated in the semiconductor complex that had just suffered one of its worst sessions in more than a year. If you owned the right slice of the technology supply chain, Monday felt like a relief rally. If you owned almost anything else, the day looked far less convincing.

The clearest story from the latest U.S. stock market session is not a broad "all clear" signal. It is a selective rebound after Friday's technology-led shakeout — one that was powered by bargain-hunting in battered chip names, a reported blockbuster manufacturing deal, and a modest easing in Middle East tensions, all while interest-rate anxiety and weak market breadth kept the recovery on a short leash.

## What happened (bullet facts with numbers)

- The **S&P 500** rose **0.30%** to close at **7,405.73**, according to CNBC. That gain only partially reversed last week's pressure tied to strong employment data and renewed fears that the Federal Reserve may need to keep policy tighter for longer.
- The **Nasdaq Composite** advanced **0.86%** to **25,929.66**, while the **Nasdaq 100** — the large-cap tech benchmark — jumped **1.6%**, its strongest session in more than a week, per Bloomberg data cited by NDTV Profit.
- The **Dow Jones Industrial Average** lagged, slipping **80.77 points**, or **0.16%**, to finish at **50,786.01**.
- **Semiconductors led the market.** The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index surged **5.6%** after plunging **10%** on Friday, when a broad chip selloff wiped out roughly **$1 trillion** in market value across U.S.-listed semiconductor companies, according to Reuters.
- **Intel** was the S&P 500's top performer, climbing about **11%** — its best day since early May — after The Information reported that Google plans to have Intel manufacture more than **3 million** custom AI chips for deployment in **2028**. Reuters noted it could not independently verify the report.
- **Micron Technology** rallied nearly **10%** after falling **13%** Friday. **Marvell Technology** gained close to **10%** on news it will join the S&P 500 effective June 22. **Broadcom** rose **2.8%** and **Nvidia** added **1.7%**, per Bloomberg.
- **Sector breadth was poor.** Eight of the S&P 500's **11 sectors** declined. The weakest groups were **utilities**, **real estate**, and **materials**. An equal-weighted version of the S&P 500 — which treats every stock the same rather than overweighting giants — finished **flat**, signaling that the benchmark's headline gain depended heavily on a handful of large names.
- **Market internals were soft.** More than **320** S&P 500 stocks fell while just over **180** rose, according to Bloomberg data cited by NDTV Profit.
- **Energy shares firmed** as oil prices steadied after reports that Iran and Israel agreed to ease strikes against each other. Major oil producers **Chevron** and **Exxon Mobil** each rose more than **1%**. Travel-related names, including **United Airlines** and **Delta Air Lines**, declined.
- **Most mega-cap tech outside chips struggled.** A basket of the largest technology companies was roughly flat. **Apple** fell **1.9%** as investors gave a lukewarm reception to its latest AI software rollout. **Microsoft** and **Alphabet** each dropped more than **1%**, while **Meta Platforms** and **Amazon** also finished lower.
- **Fear eased, but did not disappear.** The Cboe Volatility Index, or **VIX**, settled at **18.47**, down **14.1%** from Friday's close of **21.51** — the first time the gauge had topped **20** since April, a level often associated with elevated stress.
- Despite Monday's bounce, the S&P 500 remained roughly **2%** below its all-time closing high, according to Bloomberg.

## Why it moved (3 short points, everyday analogies OK)

**1. Friday's chip crash created a "rubber band" effect.**

When an asset class falls hard and fast, some investors treat the drop as an overreaction — especially if the long-term story behind the trade has not fundamentally changed. That is what happened in semiconductors. After Friday's steep decline, traders stepped back in on Monday, snapping up the same stocks that had been sold most aggressively. Think of it like a store that marks down one aisle after a panic: shoppers rush to that aisle first, even if the rest of the mall stays quiet. CNBC's analysis showed that the worst-performing stocks from Friday were among Monday's biggest gainers, a classic sign of short-term mean reversion rather than a brand-new bull thesis.

**2. A reported Intel–Google deal reframed the AI supply-chain story.**

The reported order for more than three million custom AI chips gave investors a fresh narrative beyond "AI stocks are too expensive." It suggested that hyperscale cloud companies are still racing to secure manufacturing capacity years into the future, and that U.S. chip foundry capacity may be gaining credibility as a second source alongside dominant Asian producers. That kind of headline does not guarantee future profits, but it can shift sentiment quickly in a sector that has been priced for perfection. Intel's double-digit surge was the market's way of saying the foundry turnaround story just received its most important external validation in years — even with Reuters noting the report remained unverified.

**3. Geopolitics cooled just enough to help energy, while macro worries kept the rally uneven.**

Over the weekend and into Monday, headlines around Iran and Israel shifted from escalation toward de-escalation, helping oil prices stabilize and giving energy stocks a lift. That removed one immediate source of geopolitical fuel for further panic. At the same time, stronger recent labor-market readings have revived the idea that the Fed may not be done fighting inflation — a reason bond traders are watching this week's consumer-price data closely. The result was a market that could celebrate a chip rebound without fully embracing risk everywhere else. The Dow's small loss and the flat equal-weight S&P 500 capture that tension: the index went up, but not because the whole economy suddenly looked safer.

## What to watch next (2-3 objective bullets, not trade advice)

- **Wednesday's May consumer-price index report** is the week's first major macro checkpoint. Bond markets are positioned for a firm inflation reading, and any surprise on either side could reshape expectations for Fed policy ahead of the June 16–17 meeting, when officials are widely expected to hold rates steady in the **3.50% to 3.75%** range.
- **Whether chip strength broadens or fades** will determine if Monday's move was a durable recovery or a one-day bounce. Watch whether non-chip sectors — especially financials, industrials, and consumer companies — start participating, and whether the equal-weight S&P 500 can move higher alongside the cap-weighted index.
- **Middle East headlines and oil prices** remain a live risk input. Even with Monday's calmer tone, the conflict has already run past the 100-day mark, and any renewed flare-up could quickly feed back into inflation expectations, travel stocks, and overall risk appetite.

Monday's session answered one question and left another hanging. The answer: dip buyers are still active in the AI and semiconductor trade. The unanswered question: whether the rest of the market is willing to follow them. Until breadth improves and inflation data clarify the Fed's next move, this recovery looks less like a broad turnaround and more like a targeted repair job on last week's damage.
