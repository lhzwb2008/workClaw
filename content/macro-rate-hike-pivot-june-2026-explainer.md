# Did the May Jobs Report Just Kill the Rate-Cut Trade?

**One-sentence takeaway:** A stronger-than-expected May payrolls print, combined with sticky inflation and an oil-driven energy shock, has pushed markets from pricing Federal Reserve rate cuts toward pricing rate hikes — and today's May CPI release is the last major data point before new Chair Kevin Warsh presides over his first policy meeting.

---

## What Happened: Who Said and Did What, and When

The dominant US macro storyline over the past 72 hours is not a single headline — it is a **regime shift in interest-rate expectations**. Three events chained together to create it.

### Friday, June 5: The jobs report that changed the conversation

At 8:30 a.m. ET, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the US economy added **172,000 nonfarm payroll jobs in May**, with the unemployment rate **unchanged at 4.3%**. Job gains were concentrated in leisure and hospitality (+70,000), local government (+55,000), and health care (+35,000). Financial activities lost 22,000 jobs.

The report also carried significant revisions: March payrolls were revised up by 29,000 to **214,000**, and April was revised up by 64,000 to **179,000** — a combined upward revision of **93,000** for the prior two months.

Average hourly earnings rose **12 cents (0.3%)** in May to **$37.53**, up **3.4%** over the past year, according to the BLS.

Within hours, interest-rate futures repriced sharply. Reuters reported on June 5 that CME FedWatch showed the probability of a Fed rate hike by the **December 2026** meeting rising to roughly **68%**, compared with about **52%** the prior day. CNBC reported similar figures near **70%** by midday Friday.

### June 8–9: Bond markets digested the new reality

Treasury yields remained elevated as traders absorbed the jobs data alongside ongoing oil-price volatility linked to Middle East tensions.

According to the Federal Reserve's daily H.15 release for **June 9, 2026**, the **10-year Treasury yield** stood at **4.56%**, and the **2-year yield** at **4.15%**. Bloomberg reported that two- and five-year yields had risen more than **80 basis points** from their March 2026 lows, while the 10-year had climbed more than **60 basis points** to roughly **4.55%**.

Goldman Sachs economists told Bloomberg they **no longer expect the Fed to cut rates in 2026** given labor-market strength. BNP Paribas analysts shifted to forecasting **three quarter-point rate hikes starting in December**, according to Bloomberg.

### Sunday–Tuesday: Political pushback meets market pricing

President Donald Trump, in an interview with NBC's *Meet the Press* reported by Bloomberg, said there is **"no reason" for the Fed to raise rates** and that policymakers **"should actually lower interest rates."**

That message collides with what markets are pricing. As of **June 9**, CME FedWatch data cited by Growbeansprout showed roughly a **96.5% probability** that the Fed holds its target range at **3.50% to 3.75%** at the upcoming meeting — but growing odds of tightening later in the year.

### Wednesday, June 10: The inflation verdict (scheduled)

The BLS is scheduled to release **May 2026 CPI at 8:30 a.m. ET on June 10** — six days before the **June 16–17 Federal Open Market Committee meeting**, where **Kevin Warsh** presides over his first session as Fed chair after being sworn in on **May 22, 2026**.

The most recent confirmed inflation reading is **April 2026**: headline CPI rose **0.6%** month over month (seasonally adjusted) and **3.8%** year over year; core CPI (excluding food and energy) rose **0.4%** month over month and **2.8%** year over year, per the BLS release on May 12. The energy index was up **17.9%** over the 12 months ending in April.

Market consensus ahead of the May print, as reported by multiple outlets, pointed to headline CPI potentially re-accelerating toward roughly **4.2% year over year** and core CPI edging toward **2.9%** — though prediction-market estimates for headline inflation varied more widely. **May CPI actuals: data pending verification at time of publication.**

## Transmission to Stocks: How Rates Flow Into Valuations

For retail investors, the chain from macro data to portfolio pain (or relief) runs through a few clear links.

**Step 1: Strong data → higher rate expectations.** When payrolls beat forecasts and inflation stays above the Fed's 2% target, traders reduce the odds of rate cuts and increase the odds of holds — or hikes. That shift shows up first in the **short end of the Treasury curve** (2-year yields), which tracks near-term Fed policy expectations.

**Step 2: Higher yields → higher discount rates.** Stock valuations depend partly on the rate used to discount future corporate earnings. When the 10-year Treasury yield rises — it has climbed roughly 60+ basis points from March lows, per Bloomberg — the "price of money" goes up. Future profits are worth less in today's dollars. That pressure hits **long-duration assets** hardest: high-growth technology, speculative biotech, and any company valued primarily on earnings far in the future.

**Step 3: Sector rotation.** Rising rates and inflation fears tend to favor companies with **pricing power, near-term cash flows, and lower debt loads**. They tend to punish **rate-sensitive sectors** — housing (mortgage rates track the 10-year), utilities, and real estate investment trusts — as well as companies that borrowed heavily when rates were lower.

**Step 4: The dollar and oil channel.** Reuters reported the dollar retreating from recent highs as Gulf tensions eased in early June, even as rate-hike bets rose. Oil prices near **$90+ per barrel** (Bloomberg and CNBC cited levels in this range during the period) feed directly into headline CPI through the energy component, which accounted for over **40%** of April's monthly CPI increase, per the BLS. That creates a feedback loop: geopolitical risk → higher energy → higher inflation prints → higher yields → tighter financial conditions → slower growth expectations.

Think of it like adjusting the interest rate on a mortgage calculator. A half-point increase does not change the house — but it changes what you can afford to pay. The stock market is repricing what it can "afford" to pay for corporate earnings.

## What Smart Money Is Debating (Both Sides, No Calls)

Institutional investors are not monolithic on this pivot. The debate has at least three live fault lines.

### Fault line 1: Is this a real hike cycle or a market overshoot?

**The hawkish camp** argues the May jobs report, combined with April's 3.8% headline CPI and elevated oil, means the Fed's next move after a June hold is more likely **up than down**. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan said in a recent speech — cited by CNBC — that **"higher interest rates could be necessary later this year to fully restore price stability."** Goldman and BNP Paribas have shifted forecasts accordingly.

**The skeptical camp** notes that wage growth is still moderating (3.4% year over year in May, per BLS) and that much of the inflation spike is **energy-driven and potentially temporary**. Fed Governor Michelle Bowman argued the central bank should not overreact to a possible energy-supply-shock price spike, according to CNBC. Some economists expect May CPI to show a cooler monthly pace even if the year-over-year headline rate looks elevated.

### Fault line 2: Can Kevin Warsh hold the line?

Warsh enters the chairmanship with a stated preference for lower rates and skepticism toward forward guidance — but also faces **public challenges from Fed colleagues** on his preferred inflation metrics. CNBC reported that multiple officials, including Logan and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, have pushed back on relying too heavily on "trimmed mean" inflation measures that Warsh has cited as evidence price pressures are closer to 2% than headline data suggest.

The institutional question: Can Warsh signal independence from White House pressure for cuts while keeping a divided committee aligned? His **June 16–17 press conference** will be scrutinized as much for tone as for the rate decision itself.

### Fault line 3: Strong headline, soft underbelly?

A subtler debate centers on labor-market composition. Payroll growth is solid, but **long-term unemployment** (jobless 27 weeks or more) was **2.0 million** in May — up **524,000** over the year, per the BLS household survey. Financial activities employment is down **107,000** from its May 2025 peak.

Bulls read this as a resilient economy that can tolerate restrictive policy. Bears read it as a labor market with growing cracks beneath a healthy surface — the kind of divergence that makes aggressive hiking risky.

## Three Things to Watch Next

**1. The May CPI print and market reaction (June 10).**
This is the Fed's last major inflation input before the June meeting. A headline reading near or above consensus (~4.2% year over year, per several pre-release surveys) would reinforce the hike narrative and likely push yields higher. A meaningful downside surprise could reopen cut speculation for late 2026 — at least temporarily.

**2. The June 16–17 FOMC meeting and Warsh's press conference.**
Markets expect no rate change, but the **Summary of Economic Projections dot plot** and statement language matter enormously. Watch whether the median 2026 projection shifts from one cut (March SEP) to zero cuts — or includes dissenting dots pointing toward hikes. Warsh's comments on inflation, oil, and Fed independence will set the tone for the second half of 2026.

**3. Oil prices and geopolitical developments.**
Bloomberg identified the oil-price surge following the late-February US-Iran conflict as a principal driver of Treasury yield moves. Energy remains the wildcard in the inflation equation. Any escalation that pushes crude back toward recent highs near **$96** (CNBC cited WTI closing at this level on June 3) would complicate the Fed's job regardless of what domestic labor data show.
