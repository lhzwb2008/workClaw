# Is Kevin Warsh's First Fed Meeting About to Kill the Rate-Cut Dream — or Did the Iran Deal Just Give Him an Exit Ramp?

**Takeaway:** As Kevin Warsh opens his first Federal Open Market Committee meeting on Tuesday, June 16, markets have stopped asking when the Fed will cut and started asking how long policy will stay on hold — or whether a hike is next — after May inflation hit 4.2% and wholesale prices surged 6.5%, only to be partially softened by a U.S.–Iran peace announcement on Monday, June 15, that sent oil and Treasury yields lower hours before the most consequential Fed gathering of 2026.

---

## What Happened

The dominant U.S. macro narrative of the past 72 hours is a regime shift in how markets price Federal Reserve policy, arriving as a new chair takes the microphone.

On **Wednesday, June 10**, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the Consumer Price Index rose **0.5%** in May, lifting the **12-month headline rate to 4.2%** — the highest since **April 2023** and up from **3.8%** in April. Energy prices jumped **3.9%** for the month and **23.5%** over the year. Core CPI rose a milder **0.2%** on the month and **2.9%** year over year, below the **0.3%** monthly consensus tracked by Dow Jones.

That print landed alongside a labor market that refuses to cool. On **Friday, June 5**, the BLS reported **172,000** nonfarm payroll jobs added in May, with the **unemployment rate steady at 4.3%**. March and April gains were revised higher by a combined **93,000**, according to Reuters.

Then the pipeline caught fire. On **Thursday, June 11**, the Producer Price Index for final demand rose **1.1%** in May — above the **0.7%** consensus — pushing the **12-month wholesale rate to 6.5%**, the largest annual increase since **November 2022**.

The market response was swift. CNBC reported that futures markets after the CPI release began pricing the **next Fed move as a hike rather than a cut**, with December as the most likely timing. On **June 8**, Goldman Sachs pushed its forecast for the final two rate cuts from late 2026 into **June and December 2027**, and raised the probability of a modest hike to **20%**, up from **10%**. CME FedWatch data in mid-June showed roughly **98%** probability of an unchanged decision at this meeting and approximately **70%** probability of at least one **25-basis-point** increase by year-end.

Then geopolitics rewrote the setup. On **Monday, June 15**, President Donald Trump announced that the United States and Iran had reached a peace agreement to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Formal signing is scheduled for **Friday, June 19**, in Switzerland. Reuters reported Brent crude fell roughly **$4** to about **$82.96** per barrel, while U.S. benchmark crude dropped to about **$80.35**. The **10-year Treasury yield** declined to roughly **4.43%**, its lowest level in about a month, after closing near **4.47%** on **Friday, June 12**.

The FOMC convened **Tuesday, June 16**, for a two-day meeting — the first chaired by **Kevin Warsh**, sworn in on **May 22** after a **54–45** Senate confirmation. The policy decision and updated dot plot are scheduled **Wednesday, June 17, at 2:00 p.m. Eastern Time**, with Warsh's debut press conference at **2:30 p.m. Eastern Time**. The federal funds target range has been held at **3.50% to 3.75%** since **December 2025**. A Reuters survey of **102 economists** found unanimous expectation of a hold.

## How This Reaches Your Portfolio (The Transmission Chain)

For equity investors, the transmission runs through interest rates first.

**Rates and valuations.** When long-term Treasury yields rise, the discount rate on future earnings rises too, compressing price-to-earnings multiples — especially for growth companies whose profits sit far in the future. The June 15 yield drop offered brief relief, but levels near **4.4% to 4.5%** remain elevated relative to the sub-4% environment that supported much of the prior rally.

**Inflation and margins.** Headline inflation at **4.2%** with wholesale prices at **6.5%** means input costs are climbing faster than many businesses can absorb. Resilient job growth supports near-term spending, but sustained energy inflation erodes real purchasing power over time.

**Credit and housing.** The 10-year yield benchmarks mortgage rates and corporate borrowing. A market that has shifted from pricing cuts toward debating hikes means financing costs face sustained pressure — dampening housing affordability and debt-funded expansion.

**The dollar and global earnings.** Higher U.S. rates tend to support the dollar, reducing the home-currency value of overseas revenue for multinational companies. The June 15 yield decline partially reversed recent dollar strength, but the broader trend this year has favored the greenback as the Fed's path diverged from cutting cycles abroad.

**Sector rotation.** Energy producers benefited from the oil spike; the June 15 crude retreat partially reverses that. Airlines, logistics, and consumer businesses get relief when oil falls. Financials can benefit from higher rates if loan demand holds, but suffer if yields reflect inflation fear rather than growth optimism.

## What Smart Money Is Debating (Both Sides)

**Has the narrative permanently shifted from cuts to holds — or hikes?** Hawks point to a third consecutive month of headline inflation acceleration, payrolls nearly doubling some forecasts, and major banks pushing cut timelines into 2027. Doves counter that core CPI rose just **0.2%** in May, shelter inflation moderated to **0.3%** monthly, and the June 15 oil retreat — if sustained — could make summer inflation prints look very different from May's snapshot.

**Does the Iran deal change the Fed's math?** Optimists say yes: if crude stabilizes near **$80 to $83** and Hormuz shipping resumes, the energy component that drove **60%** of May's CPI increase could fade within one or two cycles, giving Warsh room to emphasize patience. Skeptics note conflicting accounts from both governments, no signed text as of June 16, and sticky categories like shelter and medical care that will not reverse with a diplomatic headline alone.

**What will Warsh signal Wednesday?** The hold is nearly fully priced. The debate is communication — whether the FOMC removes language that has functioned as a soft easing bias, whether the dot plot pushes cut projections into 2027, and whether even one policymaker pencils in a 2026 hike. Warsh told senators he favors open debate; his press conference will reveal whether that means a divided, data-dependent message or a chair who sets a clear directional tone.

---

## Three Things to Watch Next

**1. The June 17 FOMC statement, dot plot, and Warsh press conference.** The hold is expected. Markets will parse every word for whether the committee drops its easing bias and how many dots shift toward hikes.

**2. Whether the June 19 U.S.–Iran signing holds — and where oil settles.** A formal memorandum could extend the oil and yield decline. A collapse would re-embed geopolitical risk premium at the worst possible moment for the Fed.

**3. June and July inflation and labor data.** The next CPI release is scheduled for **July 14**; June payrolls arrive **July 2**. If headline inflation decelerates while core holds near **3%**, the transitory-shock thesis gains traction. If PPI pressure feeds through despite lower oil, the higher-for-longer consensus deepens regardless of Wednesday's messaging.

---

The macro picture entering June 16 is defined by one unresolved tension: hard inflation data arguing for an extended pause or tightening, and a fragile geopolitical breakthrough arguing for patience. The Fed is not expected to move rates Wednesday. But after a week that saw wholesale inflation hit its highest level in three years and crude swing on diplomacy headlines, Kevin Warsh's first meeting will matter less for the decision itself than for whether it closes — or reopens — the door to rate cuts for the rest of 2026.
