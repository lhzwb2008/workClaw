# Is Kevin Warsh About to Close the Door on Rate Cuts at His First Fed Meeting?

**Takeaway:** The Federal Reserve is widely expected to hold rates steady on June 17, but Kevin Warsh's inaugural FOMC meeting matters less for the decision itself than for whether the committee abandons its easing bias and signals a higher-for-longer path—just as a U.S.–Iran framework deal sends oil and Treasury yields lower, reopening the inflation debate.

---

## What Happened

The defining U.S. macro story of the past 72 hours is not a single number. It is a policy inflection point: Kevin Warsh presides over his first Federal Open Market Committee meeting on June 16–17, 2026, with markets, economists, and politicians all asking the same question—does the Fed still see rate cuts ahead, or has the inflation shock from the Iran war forced a harder reset?

**Warsh takes the chair at a charged moment.** Warsh was sworn in as the 17th Federal Reserve chair on May 22, 2026, succeeding Jerome Powell. The two-day FOMC session opened on Monday, June 16. The rate decision, updated Summary of Economic Projections (including the dot plot), and Warsh's debut press conference are scheduled for Wednesday, June 17, at 2:00 p.m. and 2:30 p.m. Eastern Time, respectively. Reuters reported on June 15 that investors will treat this as Warsh's first substantive comments on inflation, unemployment, and the economic outlook—not the abstract policy commentary he offered before taking office.

**The data backdrop turned hawkish before the meeting began.** On June 10, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the Consumer Price Index rose 0.5% in May on a seasonally adjusted basis, lifting year-over-year headline inflation to 4.2%—the highest annual pace in more than three years. The energy index accounted for more than 60% of the monthly increase and is up 23.5% over the past year; gasoline prices rose 7.0% in May alone. Core CPI, excluding food and energy, rose a more contained 0.2% for the month and 2.9% year-over-year.

Five days earlier, on June 5, the May employment report showed nonfarm payrolls rising by 172,000, with the unemployment rate unchanged at 4.3% and average hourly earnings up 3.4% year-over-year. The combination—a resilient labor market plus sticky headline inflation—gave Fed officials less reason to signal future easing.

**Then geopolitics shifted the near-term calculus.** On June 15, President Donald Trump said the United States and Iran had reached a memorandum of understanding aimed at ending the war and reopening the Strait of Hormuz, the waterway through which roughly one-fifth of global oil flows before the conflict. Oil prices fell sharply: Brent crude settled down 4.76% at $83.17 per barrel and West Texas Intermediate down 4.87% at $80.75, both at roughly three-month lows, according to market reports. Treasury yields eased in response; CNBC reported the 10-year yield falling to about 4.47% and the 2-year yield to about 4.06% on June 15, as traders trimmed expectations for near-term Fed tightening.

**The hold itself is not in doubt.** The federal funds target range has stood at 3.50%–3.75% since December 2025. Multiple outlets, citing CME FedWatch and economist surveys, put the probability of no change at this meeting near consensus levels. What markets are pricing instead is a language shift: dropping statement wording that the next move is likely to be a cut, in favor of neutral guidance that keeps hikes on the table. At the April 28–29 meeting, three policymakers dissented in favor of that change; Reuters noted that Fed Governor Christopher Waller and others have since indicated support after May's hiring rebound eased labor-market concerns.

At the March 2026 meeting, the median dot plot pointed to a 3.4% federal funds rate at year-end 2026—implying roughly one quarter-point cut from the current range. Analysts widely expect that projection to be revised away at this meeting, with the median showing rates on hold through 2026.

## How This Reaches Stocks (In Plain English)

For equity investors, the June meeting is primarily a **valuation and duration** story—not a binary "hold or cut" event.

Interest rates act as the discount rate on future corporate profits. When the Fed holds policy steady but removes language pointing toward cuts—and especially if the dot plot shows fewer expected easings—investors revise upward their estimate of how long borrowing costs will stay elevated. That raises the discount applied to future earnings, which tends to compress price-to-earnings multiples. Companies whose value depends heavily on profits far in the future—many growth-oriented businesses—typically feel this most.

Higher or stickier rates also keep financing costs elevated for households and corporations. Mortgage rates, auto loans, and corporate credit remain tied to the level of policy rates and Treasury yields. When the Fed signals that relief is not imminent, consumer spending on big-ticket items and business investment in expansion can soften. That creates headwinds for cyclical sectors tied to the real economy, even if the labor market remains solid.

The June 15 oil drop adds a cross-current. Cheaper crude, if sustained, would eventually feed through to gasoline and headline inflation—potentially giving the Fed room to stay patient rather than hike. That is partly why Treasury yields fell even as the FOMC meeting approached. For stocks, lower energy costs help consumers and reduce input expenses for many businesses, but they also change the inflation narrative the Fed must address on Wednesday.

The net effect: equity markets are less sensitive to the hold itself than to **how long "higher for longer" lasts** and whether Warsh's communication style—he has criticized heavy forward guidance and questioned the dot plot's usefulness—introduces new uncertainty around interpreting Fed signals.

## What Smart Money Is Debating

**The hawkish camp** argues the Fed cannot look dovish with headline inflation at 4.2%, hiring at 172,000 in May, and unemployment at 4.3%. Proponents expect the committee to drop its easing bias, revise the dot plot to show no 2026 cuts, and potentially mark up inflation projections. Reuters cited economists noting that standard policy rules now almost uniformly suggest rates should rise—a tension Warsh must navigate without explicitly committing to hikes. Some point to Warsh's historical preference for tighter policy during past crises and his interest in balance-sheet reduction as signs financial conditions could tighten even without a rate move.

**The patient / optionality camp** counters that much of the inflation spike is energy-driven and potentially reversible. Core CPI at 2.9% year-over-year has not broken out. The U.S.–Iran framework deal, if implemented, could pull oil lower over coming weeks, though analysts from the Energy Information Administration and major banks caution that reopening the Strait of Hormuz and restoring pre-conflict tanker traffic may take months—and global inventories have been drawn down sharply during the war. Natixis economist Christopher Hodge told Reuters he still expects eventual cuts rather than hikes, arguing Warsh may adopt a "neutral-to-hawkish tone" without precluding easings if the energy shock proves transitory.

**The communication and independence camp** focuses on Warsh himself. He has said he favors less forward guidance and more open internal debate. Investors wonder whether he will de-emphasize the dot plot, submit his own projection, or face a split message if the statement turns neutral while individual dots show hike expectations. Reuters also highlighted the political dimension: President Trump has pushed for lower rates, while bond markets through mid-June had been pricing meaningful odds of hikes by year-end—leaving Warsh caught between competing pressures.

Neither camp has a clear edge before Wednesday's release. That ambiguity is why this meeting dominates macro conversation.

## Three Things to Watch Next

1. **The policy statement and dot plot (Wednesday, June 17, ~2:00 p.m. ET).** Does the FOMC remove language indicating the next move is likely a cut? Does the median 2026 dot shift from implying a cut to showing rates on hold—or higher? An upward revision to inflation projections without a matching rate path would raise questions about whether the Fed is repeating past mistakes by treating price shocks as temporary.

2. **Warsh's debut press conference (Wednesday, ~2:30 p.m. ET).** His first Q&A as chair will set expectations for how he frames inflation, energy, tariffs, and labor-market strength. Listen for whether he treats the Iran-driven oil decline as disinflationary relief or insufficient given how far headline CPI remains above the 2% target.

3. **Iran deal follow-through and oil supply (through late June and beyond).** A formal signing is reportedly scheduled for June 19 in Switzerland. Whether the Strait of Hormuz reopens on the timeline described in the memorandum—and how quickly oil flows normalize—will determine if the June 15 drop in yields and hike expectations persists or reverses before the Fed's next meetings.
