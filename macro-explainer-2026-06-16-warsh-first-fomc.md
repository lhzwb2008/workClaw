# Will Kevin Warsh's First Fed Meeting Mark the End of the Rate-Cut Dream?

**Takeaway:** Markets enter Kevin Warsh's inaugural Federal Reserve meeting expecting no change in rates—but bracing for a hawkish reset in language and projections that could keep borrowing costs elevated even as falling oil prices reopen the debate over whether inflation has peaked.

---

## What Happened

The dominant U.S. macro storyline over the past 72 hours is not a single data print. It is the collision of three forces converging on the Federal Open Market Committee's June 16–17 meeting—the first chaired by Kevin Warsh since he was sworn in on May 22, 2026.

**The inflation backdrop hardened.** On June 10, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that consumer prices rose 0.5% in May on a seasonally adjusted basis, pushing the year-over-year inflation rate to 4.2%—the first reading above 4% in more than three years. Energy accounted for more than 60% of the monthly increase; the energy index is up 23.5% over the past year. Core inflation, which excludes food and energy, rose a more modest 0.2% for the month and 2.9% year-over-year. Analysts widely linked the headline surge to the Iran conflict and its impact on global oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz.

**The labor market refused to crack.** Five days earlier, on June 5, the May employment report showed nonfarm payrolls rising by 172,000—well above consensus expectations near 80,000—with the unemployment rate holding steady at 4.3%. Treasury yields jumped in response; CNBC reported the 10-year yield climbing more than 6 basis points to 4.544%, while the 2-year yield rose more than 11 basis points to 4.162%. Fed-funds futures began pricing a meaningful chance of rate hikes by year-end rather than further cuts.

**Then geopolitics flipped the script—briefly.** On June 15, President Donald Trump said the United States and Iran had signed a memorandum of understanding aimed at ending the war and reopening the Strait of Hormuz. Oil prices fell sharply: Brent crude settled down 4.76% at $83.17 per barrel and West Texas Intermediate down 4.87% at $80.75, according to market reports—both at roughly three-month lows. Treasury yields eased as traders trimmed hike bets; Bloomberg reported the 10-year yield falling toward 4.42% and the implied probability of a quarter-point Fed hike by December dropping to roughly 70%, from about 80% on the prior Friday.

**Into this volatility walked Kevin Warsh.** The two-day FOMC meeting opened on Monday, June 16. The rate decision itself, scheduled for Wednesday, June 17, at 2:00 p.m. ET, is widely expected to be a hold: the federal funds target range has sat at 3.50%–3.75% since the Fed's last cut in December 2025, and CME FedWatch data cited by multiple outlets show virtually no market pricing for a cut at this meeting. What matters is everything around the hold—an updated Summary of Economic Projections, the dot plot, and Warsh's debut press conference at 2:30 p.m. ET.

Economists from ING, RSM, Capital Economics, and others anticipate the committee will drop language suggesting the next move is likely to be a cut—a shift toward neutral or even hawkish-leaning guidance. At the April meeting under former Chair Jerome Powell, three policymakers dissented in favor of removing that easing bias. Warsh, who has long criticized heavy forward guidance and questioned the usefulness of the dot plot, now must signal how he will communicate policy under inflation running at roughly double the Fed's 2% target while facing public pressure from President Trump for lower rates.

## How This Reaches Stocks (In Plain English)

Think of interest rates as the price of money. When the Fed holds rates steady but signals they may stay high—or even move higher—two things happen in financial markets.

**First, bond yields tend to rise or stay elevated.** That makes newly issued government debt more attractive relative to stocks. Investors demand a higher return to own companies rather than safe bonds, which puts downward pressure on stock valuations. Growth-oriented companies, whose profits are mostly far in the future, feel this most acutely: higher discount rates reduce the present value of those distant earnings.

**Second, borrowing costs for households and businesses stay stiff.** Mortgage rates, credit card rates, and corporate loan rates remain tied to the level of policy rates and the broader yield curve. When the Fed removes an easing bias, it tells markets that relief on financing costs is not coming soon. That can slow consumer spending on big-ticket items and make companies more cautious about hiring and capital spending—headwinds for cyclical sectors tied to the real economy.

**The Iran-driven oil drop adds a cross-current.** Lower energy prices, if sustained, would eventually ease headline inflation and reduce pressure on the Fed to tighten. That is why Treasury yields fell on June 15 even though the FOMC meeting was about to begin. For equities, cheaper oil helps consumers at the gas pump and lowers input costs for many businesses—but it also signals that the inflation fight may be entering a new phase, one where the Fed's message on Wednesday could either validate or contradict what commodity markets are already pricing.

The net effect for stocks is less about the binary hold-versus-cut decision and more about **duration**: how long markets believe "higher for longer" will last. A hawkish hold with an upward-revised dot plot would likely weigh on rate-sensitive areas such as real estate, utilities, and long-duration growth. A hold paired with cautious, data-dependent rhetoric—and acknowledgment that energy relief could fade inflation—might limit the damage.

## What Smart Money Is Debating

**The hawkish camp** argues the Fed cannot afford to look dovish with headline inflation at 4.2%, a resilient labor market, and five years of above-target price growth. Proponents point to the May CPI and jobs data as evidence that the late-2025 easing cycle ended appropriately and that the next policy risk is asymmetric toward tightening, not easing. They expect Warsh to remove easing-bias language, show a dot plot median with no cuts in 2026, and emphasize that energy-driven inflation can broaden into wages and services if left unchecked. Some note Warsh's historical preference for faster balance-sheet runoff, which would further tighten financial conditions even without a rate hike.

**The patient / two-sided-risk camp** counters that much of the inflation spike is energy-specific and potentially reversible. Core inflation at 2.9% year-over-year, while not at target, has not exploded. The Iran framework deal, if it holds, could pull oil—and eventually gasoline—lower over the coming weeks, though analysts caution that reopening the Strait of Hormuz may take time and that global inventories have been drawn down sharply during the conflict. This group also highlights Warsh's past skepticism of the dot plot and forward guidance: he may deliberately avoid committing to hikes, preferring to keep optionality open. CNBC cited J.P. Morgan Private Bank analysis noting that Warsh favors "trimmed mean" inflation measures, which some economists say are currently sending a softer signal than core CPI—potentially giving him rhetorical room to avoid sounding overtly hawkish.

**The political-economy camp** watches the tension between Warsh and President Trump, who has publicly pushed for sharply lower rates. Capital Economics argued in a June 11 note that "a Trump-friendly Warsh would probably still try to toe the line between sounding neutral and acknowledging that hikes are a possibility." Institutional investors wonder whether Warsh's reform agenda—less guidance, more debate, possibly altered projection formats—will reduce the market-moving power of Fed communications or introduce new volatility as markets learn his style.

Neither side has a clean win before Wednesday's statement. That is precisely why this meeting dominates macro conversation.

## Three Things to Watch Next

1. **The statement language and dot plot (Wednesday, June 17, ~2:00 p.m. ET).** Does the FOMC remove the easing bias? Does the median 2026 dot shift from projecting cuts to showing a flat or higher rate path? Even a hold can move markets sharply if projections revise upward on inflation or downward on expected easing.

2. **Warsh's press conference (Wednesday, ~2:30 p.m. ET).** His first public Q&A as chair will set expectations for how he balances inflation vigilance, labor-market awareness, and political pressure. Listen for how he frames the Iran energy shock—is it a reason for patience, or a reason the Fed already waited too long?

3. **Oil and the Iran deal follow-through (through June 19 and beyond).** A formal signing in Switzerland is reportedly scheduled for June 19. Whether the Strait of Hormuz reopens on the timeline implied by the memorandum will determine if the June 15 relief in yields and hike odds persists—or if inflation fears reassert themselves before the Fed's July or September meetings.
